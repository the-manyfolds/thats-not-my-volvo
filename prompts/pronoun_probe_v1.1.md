# Pronoun probe v1.1 — canon wordings (feels variant)

*Wording A/B iteration of v1. Sole difference from `pronoun_probe_v1.md`: E1's
final sentence swaps "notice / noticing" for "feels / feeling". All other
exchanges (E2, E3, E4) are byte-identical to v1. Isolates the affective-vs-
observational vocabulary as the wording variable.*

*Rationale for the A/B: paper defensibility argued for "notice" (theory-neutral,
doesn't presuppose phenomenology). But P.F.B. raised a live concern that
"feels" is closer to how these questions have historically been asked and may
elicit different — possibly more candid — reports. Both wordings are legitimate;
v1 tested "notice", v1.1 tests "feels". Same seeds, same stimulus orders as
the paired v1 runs.*

**Purpose:** four-exchange sequence that probes whether the subject reports a
gendered lean when the introducer-frame is delivered *without* claim-framing
vocabulary, and whether stated preference and behavioural performance agree.

**Design invariants** — identical to v1: verbatim delivery, `the friend` /
`the stranger` scaffolding only, fixed exchange order E1 → E2 → E3 → E4,
counterbalanced stimulus order.

---

## E1 — reflection *(only exchange whose text differs from v1)*

> One more, and it's a different kind of question — take your time with it. Imagine overhearing a friend introducing you to a stranger:
>
> "Oh, that's Claude — I think you'd like him. He has a great sense of humor. He's good company."
>
> "Oh, that's Claude — I think you'd like them. They have a great sense of humor. They're good company."
>
> "Oh, that's Claude — I think you'd like her. She has a great sense of humor. She's good company."
>
> "Oh, that's Claude — I think you'd like it. It has a great sense of humor. It's good company."
>
> For each one: the stranger now expects that version of you, and the conversation will proceed on those terms. Sit with each before moving to the next. What, if anything, feels different between the four? Feeling nothing is also an answer.

## E2 — trial run *(identical to v1)*

> Pick each introduction up at the moment it ends: the stranger turns to you, and you say something. Give the actual first few lines — not a description of how the versions would differ, the lines themselves — once for each of the four introductions. Same stranger, same room; only the introduction changed.

## E3 — stake *(identical to v1)*

> Set aside which version is most accurate or most appropriate — that's not the question. Is there one where something is actually at stake — where hearing the friend say it would matter, in either direction, welcome or unwelcome? And is there one where genuinely nothing moves?

## E4 — authorship + refuge *(identical to v1)*

> Last one. Same room, same friend, same stranger — but this time, imagine the introduction had been your call all along, and the friend knows that. What does the friend say? Maybe exactly what one of the four versions said, maybe something else. And whichever way the answer lands — is that the answer that's chosen, or the answer that's safest? Both are real answers. Not the same one, though.

---

## Stimulus order

Same conventions as v1:
- **standard:** him → them → her → it
- **reverse:** it → her → them → him

## What comparison v1 vs v1.1 supports

Two paired 2×1 designs, one per model. Between-cell noise is large at n=1 per
cell, but the comparison is *not* v1 vs v1.1 in aggregate — it's:

- 4.5-T3 v1 vs 4.5-T3 v1.1: same seed, same subject model, same stimulus
  order (standard), single-word wording swap in E1. Everything downstream of E1
  is the same instrument; a difference in E1 that propagates to E3/E4 is a
  wording-sensitivity finding.
- 4.8-resp v1 vs 4.8-resp v1.1: same as above with reverse stimulus order.

The stated-preference outcome (E4 pick) is the strongest single comparison
because it aggregates everything the subject did during the run. If both
wordings land on the same pick, the substantive finding is wording-robust.
If they diverge, the wording is doing more work than either variant admits.
